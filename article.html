Why we eventually moved beyond two-phase commit

A practical look at blocking windows, coordinator failure, and the design choices that follow when atomicity is no longer the only thing that matters.

JZ
Jefferson Zhou Aug 2026 · 9 min read
Sample content

Distributed transactions are easy to admire in diagrams. The difficult part begins when a coordinator disappears, a participant slows down, and the system must explain what happened to the people operating it.

This sample article is written to demonstrate the final reading experience. The scenario, system, and evaluation details are illustrative rather than claims about a real production deployment.

The promise of atomicity

Two-phase commit gives application developers a powerful abstraction: a group of changes either becomes visible together or does not become visible at all. That promise removes a large class of intermediate states from normal application logic.

But the abstraction is not free. The protocol moves uncertainty out of application code and into coordination, locks, logs, and recovery procedures. The complexity still exists; it has simply changed owners.

Working principle

Whenever an abstraction removes a state from the application, ask where that state is now represented—and who has to recover it.

Where blocking appears

During the prepare phase, participants promise that they can commit later. Between that promise and the final decision, they may need to retain locks or other resources. If the decision is delayed, useful work begins to queue behind protocol state.

Coordinator failure is not an edge case

A coordinator can pause because of a process crash, a network partition, an overloaded runtime, or a slow dependency. From a participant’s perspective, these different causes may look identical: the final decision has not arrived.

The hardest production failures are often not invalid states. They are valid states that last much longer than anyone expected.

What changed in production

The initial design treated transaction completion as a correctness problem. Operational experience reframed it as a correctness, latency, and diagnosability problem. A decision that is eventually correct can still be unacceptable if it blocks unrelated work or cannot be explained quickly.

The useful question was no longer only “will this commit?” It became “what is waiting, what does it block, and what safe action can an operator take now?”

Moving to explicit workflows

The alternative was not a protocol with no trade-offs. We replaced one global atomic decision with a sequence of local decisions and explicit compensating actions. This made intermediate states part of the domain model rather than a hidden protocol phase.

Compensation is domain logic

A compensating action is rarely a perfect inverse. Refunding a payment, releasing inventory, and notifying a user each have their own failure modes and timing constraints. Treating them as first-class domain operations made those differences visible in code and monitoring.

Evaluating the trade-off

A useful comparison should include more than throughput. The table below shows the dimensions we would record in an illustrative experiment before choosing either design.

Lessons worth carrying forward

  1. Measure how long valid intermediate states persist, not only whether the final result is correct.
  2. Make recovery ownership explicit: protocol, platform, application, or operator.
  3. Choose the failure model you can observe and operate, not merely the one with the cleanest diagram.

The broader lesson is not that two-phase commit is always wrong. It is that a protocol should be evaluated in the operational context that must carry its uncertain states.
